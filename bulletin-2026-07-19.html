The Liturgy of the Eucharist (Part 2)

Bread and Wine are brought to the Altar (either in procession or simply placed there by the server or the priest). There are several vessels and linen cloths which are used at this point of the Mass, and it might be useful to name and explain each of these before looking at the actual ritual words and actions that accompany the offertory and preparation of the gifts. In different parishes the vessels and linens used vary, so we will just focus on the essential ones. These items include Chalice, Paten, Ciborium, Corporal, Purificator, Pall, Lavabo Bowl. Each of these liturgical items have a special blessing upon them before they are first used in the Mass. This blessing means that they are not to be used for any other purpose outside of the holy context of the Liturgy of the Mass. The Church has always taken seriously how these objects are sacred because of the immense holiness for which they are commissioned. As the Code of Canon Law (#1171) puts it: 'Sacred objects, which are designated for Divine Worship by dedication or blessing, are to be treated reverently and are not to be employed for profane or inappropriate use even if they are owned by private persons.'

The Chalice and Paten are the principal sacred vessels – one will contain the wine which will become the Precious Blood of Christ and the other will hold the Host that will become the Body of Christ. The Church requires that both these vessels be made of precious and worthy material. Chalice should not be made from glass or pottery, but of a sufficiently precious metal which is non-porous and is sturdy enough to withstand any accidental dropping. In circumstances of great poverty or in time of persecution a pewter chalice may be permitted, but the bowl of this must be gilt. Pewter is a tin alloy. Patens should not be some more or less ornate ordinary plate, but, given its purpose, a Paten should also ideally be made of a precious metal. The same would be true of the Ciborium which would contain all the smaller Hosts which will be consecrated and distributed to the faithful at Mass, and which is used to store in the Tabernacle any of the remaining hosts at the end of Mass. The Ciborium should have a lid, more or less ornate, and traditionally this lid will have a simple cross on top of it. Additionally, often a special veil is placed over the Ciborium when in the Tabernacle; veiling being traditionally a way in which the Church denotes that something is Sacred.

Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous: 1. We admitted we were powerless over alcohol-that our lives had become unmanageable. 2. Came to believe that a Power greater than ourselves could restore us to sanity. 3. Made a decision to turn our will and our lives over to the care of God as we understood Him. 4. Made a searching and fearless moral inventory of ourselves. 5. Admitted to God, to ourselves, and to another human being the exact nature of our wrongs. 6. Were entirely ready to have God remove all these defects of character. 7. Humbly asked Him to remove our shortcomings. 8. Made a list of all persons we had harmed, and became willing to make amends to them all. 9. Made direct amends to such people wherever possible, except when to do so would injure them or others. 10. Continued to take personal inventory and when we were wrong promptly admitted it. 11. Sought through prayer and meditation to improve our conscious contact with God as we understood Him, praying only for knowledge of His will for us and the power to carry that out. 12. Having had a spiritual awakening as the result of these steps, we tried to carry this message to alcoholics and to practise these principles in all our affairs.

Gratitude invites us to notice God's loving presence in the ordinary moments of each day.

Whether shared in a kind word, a quiet sunrise, or the simple gift of time with those we love, these everyday blessings remind us that grace is never far away.

As we cultivate thankful hearts, we begin to see that even the smallest moments can become encounters with God's generous love, drawing us closer to him and inspiring us to share that love with others. A grateful heart sees God's hand in everything, the laughter of a friend, the gift of a new day, even the challenges that help us grow.

The Meaning and Symbolism of the Brown Scapular

The Scapular is a symbol of Mary's protection. It is a reduced form of the Carmelite

habit. It involves 'putting on Christ' in our lives and entrusting ourselves to Mary's

protection. It includes the idea of consecration to Mary. Consecration is properly an act

done by God, so that when we say we consecrate ourselves to God and to Mary, we are

principally stating that we freely want God's will to be done in our lives. We want the

Lord Jesus to rule all we say and do. The wearing of the Scapular is a sign that we

want the values lived out by Mary to be evident in our actions and dispositions.

The First Five Saturdays - To begin from the month of August

Then our Lady said to Lucy at Fatima;

"Look, my daughter, at my Heart surrounded with thorns with which ungrateful men pierce me at every moment by their blasphemies & ingratitude...

You at least, must try to console me & say I promise to assist at the hour of death, with the graces necessary for salvation, all those who on the first Saturday of five consecutive months, shall confess, receive Holy Communion, recite five decades of the Rosary & keep me company for fifteen minutes while meditating on the fifteen mysteries of the Rosary, with the intention of making reparation."

How to practice this Devotion

Our Lady asks us on five successive First Saturdays of the month to;

1 Go to Confession

This Sacramental Confession may be made within eight days, or in case of difficulty even later, provided we are in a state of grace when we receive Our Lord.

2 Receive Holy Communion

3 Pray five decades of the Rosary

Our lady asks us to say after each decade the prayer; O Jesus, forgive us our sins, save us from the fire of hell. Lead all souls to heaven, especially those in most need of Your mercy.

4 Keep Our Lady company for fifteen minutes thinking with her about the mysteries of the Rosary.

5 Have the intention of making reparation to the Immaculate Heart of Mary.

19Ú IÚIL 2026 - AN SÉÚ DOMHNACH DÉAG - A

Reflection

In today's Gospel, Jesus offers three parables to describe the Kingdom of Heaven.

The first parable alerts us to the two-fold reality of the Kingdom of Heaven.

The beginnings of the Kingdom of Heaven can be found in this world. The fruition of the Kingdom of Heaven, however, will not be realised until the final judgment. In the meantime, as Jesus' explanation to the disciples cautions any effort to judge the progress of the Kingdom of Heaven is premature. Only God, in the final judgment, will distinguish the fruit of the Kingdom of Heaven and offer its reward.

The second and third parables call to our attention the abundance that will result from the small beginnings of the Kingdom of Heaven. Just as a mustard seed—the smallest of all seeds—will become a large bush, so too God will bring his Kingdom to full bloom. As a small amount of yeast will leaven the entire batch of bread, so too God will bring about the expansion of his Kingdom. In each case the image is of the superabundance that God brings out of even the smallest of signs of the Kingdom.

Contained within these parables are words of caution as well as words of consolation. In the parable of the sower we are warned against judging others. To judge and uproot the 'weeds' prematurely will harm the wheat; final judgment rests with God. In the parables of the mustard seed and the yeast, we are consoled by the message that God can work wonders and produce abundance from even the smallest beginnings of the Kingdom of Heaven.

Be Enriched by the Word

In 2009, the first Darkness Into Light

event in aid of Pieta House took place in

Dublin's Phoenix Park. Around 400

people walked the 5km course that year

to raise funds for the suicide prevention

charity. Fast forward to May this year,

and more than 130,000 people took part

in 150 Darkness Into Light events across

Ireland and worldwide. Many of us will

know someone who took part, or we

may have got involved ourselves this

year. As the crucial message of hope and

solidarity filters out from these events,

who knows how many lives have been

saved or transformed over the years?

Darkness Into Light is a great example

of a movement which has grown from a

small seed of an idea into a major force

for good. The parable of the mustard

seed, which we hear today, shows us that

fantastic things can emerge from the

most modest of beginnings, 'the

smallest of all seeds'. The disciples who

first heard Jesus speak were ordinary

people – not the religious leaders of the

time or those in positions of power or

influence. But they were the seeds from

which the Church would grow down

through the ages. Each act of kindness or

forgiveness, each time we spread the

Good News of Jesus in our own way, we

are hastening the spread of God's

kingdom. These seeds grow and

multiply, and bear abundant fruit even if

we cannot always see the results. We

can't change the world in one go – but

we can change our world.
