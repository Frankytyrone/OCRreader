The Deep End • Alone Time

Earlier this year I read a selection of entries for a Mother’s Day competition, in which sons and daughters described why their mother deserved the prize. The entries had several themes in common – She never does anything for herself; she’s always thinking of other people; I can’t remember when she last had a break.

In today’s Gospel it is Jesus who needs a break. It is a very human Jesus that we encounter. Having learnt of the death of John the Baptist, Jesus withdraws from the crowds in a boat to a ‘deserted place’. Like any of us, he feels the need to be alone from time to time, and this is a time of particular pressure for him. He has been preaching solidly for some time, Herod is asking questions, and now he hears his cousin John has been killed.

But there is little time for rest. Seeing the crowds that follow him, Jesus feels

another very human emotion – pity. The disciples are protective of Jesus and want the people to go away, but Jesus has other ideas. It is a compassionate Jesus who addresses the hunger of his large crowd of followers.

While he needs time to reflect and pray, and we can only imagine how tired and weary he feels, he is happy to postpone his own needs it while he cares for his flock. It’s an example followed by many a parent. But it is interesting to note what follows the story of the feeding of the five thousand. Having dismissed the crowds, Jesus makes another attempt to retreat by himself, this time up a mountain. His alone time is important.

Reflection

Dear Lord, here lie in their last rest,

the people who worked on the land.

They knew the meaning of hard work.

They knew the joy and peace that is the product of labour.

Now we trust they know the peace and happiness of everlasting life with You.

These people all their lives long

laboured to supply the food and drink necessary

to sustain human life.

Now they enjoy in all its fullness the life that You, Lord,

came down to earth to give us.

Dear Lord, bless us who labour now in the fields and hills

where these dear dead have worked.

Grant that we may remember them with charity and kindness,

walking reverently in the ways that they have left behind.

Grant, too, that we may finally meet again.

From Father's Desk

Matthew 14:13-21 - Compassion That Multiplies

In this familiar account of the feeding of the five

thousand, Jesus responds to the crowds not with

frustration but with compassion. Though He had

sought a quiet place after hearing of John the Baptist's

death, He welcomed the people, healed their sick,

and met their hunger.

When the disciples saw only five loaves and two fish,

Jesus saw an opportunity for God's abundance to be

revealed. He invited the disciples to bring what little

they had, blessed it, and used it to feed everyone pre-

sent - with twelve baskets left over.

This passage reminds us that Christ is attentive to both

our spiritual and practical needs. It also encourages

us not to underestimate what God can do with the

small offerings we place in His hands. When we bring

our time, gifts, resources, and willingness to serve,

Jesus can multiply them for the blessing of many.

May we trust Him to provide, serve others with

compassion, and offer what we have

- however small - into His hands.

STEP BY STEP THROUGH THE MASS

The Liturgy of the Eucharist (Part 4)

As the priest makes the offertory gifts ready on the Altar, this action is accompanied by several prayers of offering; to which the faithful respond: "Blessed be God forever". In those prayers the priest refers to the fact that the bread and the wine are fruits of the earth and the work of human hands. In other words, the prayer acknowledges that God did not create bread and he did not create wine – though of course he could have easily done so. Bread and Wine are things which human beings have created using the God-given raw materials of sun, soil, rain, wheat, water, fire, grapes and the microbiology processes responsible for fermentation. Of course, the intelligence which led human beings to 'create' bread and wine is itself a gift of God. The point being made in these prayers is that the bread and wine offered for the Eucharist is a combined effort of the Divine and the Human – though obviously the Divine has primacy and is the foundation of all that results. The ordinary bread is destined, in this Mass, to become the "Bread of Life"; the wine placed in the chalice is destined to become "our spiritual drink."

These prayer by the priest can often be said in a quiet voice – especially when there is an accompanying hymn. However, even without a hymn, the priest may opt to say these prayers quietly. This has the advantage of giving the faithful the opportunity to quietly place their hearts spiritually upon the altar and into the chalice – uniting themselves with Christ Jesus and his offering of himself to the Father on Calvary for the good of all souls for all time – a once for all offering and sacrifice which is made sacramentally, though really, present in this particular Mass.

One small ritual action that can be easily missed at this Preparation of the Gifts is the additional of a small drop of water to the wine in the chalice. The water is added in a very small, almost insignificant, drop or two. As it drops into the wine it is assumed into the wine and is so insignificant that it makes no material difference to the quantity nor the quality of the wine. However, this ancient custom, is far from insignificant. Next week we will look at this ritual action, and accompanying prayer, to unpack some of its enormous significance for us who are trying to prayerfully and attentively participate in the Mass.
